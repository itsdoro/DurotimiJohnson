MIMO FMCW Radar

EE 4910 / 4920 · Client and faculty advisor: Dr. Mohammed Tayeb Al Qaseer · Fall 2026 – Spring 2027

Description

A compact, portable millimeter-wave radar with four dual circularly polarized antennas, sweeping 24 to 40 GHz. The team's target application is terrain mapping for farmers: autonomous farm vehicles need an accurate elevation map of their fields, and a high-resolution FMCW radar can measure ground distance through crops with millimeter-level resolution. The same radar can also see through fog and dust to detect drones, people, and vehicles.

An FPGA drives a DAC that sweeps a VCO. The VCO feeds an up-converter, whose output passes through a microstrip-to-waveguide transition and Ka-band waveguide to Vivaldi antennas. Returning signals are down-converted to an IF signal and sampled by ADCs, and signal processing extracts range and Doppler information.

My role
  • TX/RX layout designer on a seven-person team
  • Designed the schematic for the ADAR2001 TX up-converter board, including power delivery, SPI communication, 3.3 V to 1.8 V level shifting, and eight length-matched RF outputs
  • Laying out the TX up-converter PCB in KiCad
Skills gained
  • Millimeter-wave PCB design in KiCad
  • Power delivery and level shifting
  • SPI interfacing
  • Systems-level radar design
  • Large-team collaboration
Big picture
Autonomous farm vehicles work best when they know the exact shape of the ground beneath the crops. By mapping terrain with millimeter-level resolution through vegetation, this radar can help farmers run their equipment more efficiently, reducing wasted fuel, time, and inputs, and supporting a more productive, sustainable food supply. Its ability to see through fog and dust also extends to safety and security uses, such as detecting drones, people, and vehicles when cameras and lidar fail.

Brain Organoid Case Study

EE 2320: Professional and Ethical Issues · Fall 2024

Brain organoids, lab-grown tissue that resembles the human brain, offer a safer and more human-relevant alternative to animal testing and clinical trials. Some now show brain-wave activity similar to that of premature infants, which raises the question of whether continued development could produce organoids capable of suffering.

This case study weighs consequentialism against reflexive principlism and applies the four principles of beneficence, nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice. Because nonmaleficence and justice both favor restraint, it recommends a slow, deliberate pace of advancement and a clearer line for when further development becomes unethical.
